Diagnosis is confounded with submission cohort throughout GSE5281:
non-identifiability, measurement-target mismatch and calibration failure
in Alzheimer's disease transcriptomic signature transfer

ABSTRACT

Cross-dataset performance is routinely reported as evidence that a
transcriptomic disease signature generalises. We show that such evidence can
be uninterpretable in principle, using a worked example built on two heavily
reused public Alzheimer's disease expression resources.

We developed a three-gene logistic signature (ABCA6, CRLF1, TNFRSF11B) in
bulk hippocampal tissue from GSE48350 (n = 62; 49 training, 13 held out) and
applied the locked model, without refitting, to the hippocampal subset of
GSE5281 (n = 23; 10 cases, 13 controls). Apparent training ROC-AUC was 0.855,
held-out ROC-AUC 0.694, and repeated nested cross-validation yielded 0.757
(SD 0.165; 95% range 0.398-1.000). Transfer ROC-AUC was 0.662 before and
0.685 after harmonisation to the training reference, with a bootstrap
interval of 0.442-0.886 and permutation p = 0.206 and 0.145 respectively.

Audit of the deposited metadata showed that this transfer performance cannot
be read as evidence of transportable disease-associated signal. In the
hippocampal subset, all thirteen controls belong to one Gene Expression
Omnibus submission cohort and all ten cases to another; a design containing
intercept, diagnosis and submission cohort is rank deficient (23 x 3, rank 2);
and a transcriptome-wide classifier separated the two submission cohorts with
leave-one-out ROC-AUC and accuracy of 1.000. The same nesting holds across
the entire series: all 74 samples in the July 2006 submission are controls
and all 87 in the October 2007 submission are cases, in all six profiled
brain regions. GSE5281 additionally profiles laser-capture microdissected
neurons with two-round amplification, whereas GSE48350 profiles bulk tissue,
so transfer crosses a measurement boundary as well as a cohort boundary.
Consistent with this, discrimination transported while calibration did not:
mean predicted case probability in the transfer cohort was 0.003 against an
observed prevalence of 0.435, and every sample was classified as control at
the locked threshold.

Apparent external discrimination, a non-identifiable design and a collapsed
probability scale can therefore co-occur. Studies using GSE5281 as a
case-control or validation resource should state that diagnosis and
submission cohort are not separately identifiable within it, and should note
that batch-correction procedures cannot recover identifiability from a fully
collinear design.

1. INTRODUCTION


Transcriptomic classification of Alzheimer's disease from public expression
data follows a widely repeated template. Investigators combine one or more
Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) series, filter genes by differential expression
or network membership, select a compact feature set using regularised
regression or ensemble importance measures, fit a classifier, and report a
receiver operating characteristic area under the curve, commonly between 0.85
and 0.95. Where a second dataset is available, performance in that dataset is
described as external or independent validation.

Two bodies of evidence indicate that this template yields less than its
outputs suggest. First, performance estimates in omics-based machine learning
are systematically optimistic whenever analytical choices are made with any
visibility of the evaluation data. Feature selection performed outside
cross-validation folds, normalisation applied across a train-test boundary,
and repeated-subject leakage have each been shown to inflate reported
accuracy substantially [1]. Second, Alzheimer's disease biomarker claims
replicate poorly. A prominent ten-analyte plasma lipid panel reported to
predict phenoconversion with high accuracy [2] subsequently failed
independent replication, and a recent reprofiling of the AddNeuroMed/ANMerge
blood resource found that a classifier built from randomly selected genes
achieved ROC-AUC 0.732 on the legacy data but only 0.567 once technical and
cell-count artefacts were addressed, concluding that previously reported
mitochondrial expression signals in blood did not hold [3].

A third problem has received considerably less attention, and it is the
subject of this report. Optimism and non-replication are properties of
estimates. Identifiability is a property of designs. Where the disease
variable within a dataset is collinear with a technical or administrative
variable, no analysis of that dataset, however carefully cross-validated, can
separate their effects. Performance can be measured, but it cannot be
attributed.

This distinction matters because it is not repaired by the procedures the
field customarily applies. Batch-correction methods such as ComBat and
surrogate variable analysis estimate batch effects conditional on the
biological variable of interest. When batch and biology are perfectly
collinear, the conditional model is not identified, and adjustment removes
the disease contrast together with the technical one. The standard remedy is
therefore inapplicable in precisely the situation where it appears most
necessary.

Two datasets illustrate the problem with unusual clarity, because both are
heavily reused and are frequently combined with one another. GSE48350
provides bulk brain tissue from several regions including hippocampus.
GSE5281 provides laser-capture microdissected, non-tangle-bearing neurons
from six brain regions in cases and cognitively normal controls [4].
Published studies merge these two series using surrogate variable analysis
and treat the merged data as a single expression resource [5], and other
studies use GSE5281 alone as a validation cohort for signatures developed
elsewhere.

What is known, then, is that batch effects in public expression data are
common and that correction is routine; that analytic leakage inflates
performance estimates; and that discrimination and calibration are distinct
model properties. What is not known is whether the specific public datasets
on which a substantial fraction of Alzheimer's disease transcriptomic machine
learning rests actually admit identification of diagnosis effects at all, and
what a cross-dataset performance estimate means when they do not.

We addressed this by developing a compact three-gene signature in GSE48350,
locking the model, and transferring it to the GSE5281 hippocampal subset,
treating the transfer as an object of study rather than as a validation step.
We then audited the transfer cohort for design identifiability, cohort
separability, workflow provenance and measurement-target comparability, and
assessed discrimination and calibration separately.

We emphasise at the outset that the three-gene signature is not offered as a
biomarker. It serves as a vehicle. Its properties, which include modest
internal performance, unstable feature selection and no demonstrated
advantage over a two-gene reduction, are typical rather than exceptional, and
that typicality is what makes it a useful worked example.



2. METHODS


2.1 Study design

This study was designed as a methodological audit using an Alzheimer's
disease transcriptomic signature as a worked example. GSE48350 served as the
discovery dataset and the hippocampal subset of GSE5281 as the cross-dataset
transfer-test cohort. We use the term cross-dataset transfer test throughout
and deliberately avoid the term independent external validation, for reasons
established in the Results.

2.2 Datasets

The discovery dataset was GSE48350, comprising bulk hippocampal tissue
profiled on the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 array (GPL570).
Sixty-two samples were used, partitioned into a training set of 49 samples
(15 cases, 34 controls) and a held-out internal test set of 13 samples
(4 cases, 9 controls) [6].

The transfer-test cohort was the hippocampal subset of GSE5281, comprising 23
samples (10 cases, 13 controls) on the same array platform [4]. The complete
GSE5281 series contains 161 samples across six brain regions.

Two additional datasets were used for cross-modality assessment: GSE63060,
peripheral blood [7], and GSE138852, single-nucleus RNA sequencing of
entorhinal cortex comprising three case and three control libraries [8].

2.3 Preprocessing and age adjustment

Both microarray datasets were processed from CEL files using robust multi-array
average normalisation and summarised to gene level. The discovery data yielded
21,367 genes from 54,675 probes. A variance filter computed on training
samples only retained the top 25% of genes by variance, giving 5,342
candidate genes.

Age was then adjusted for, prior to feature selection, using training samples
only. For each gene g, an ordinary least-squares model of expression on age
was fitted across the 49 training samples. Adjusted values were obtained by
subtracting the fitted age effect, with age centred on the training mean in
both the training and held-out sets, so that no held-out information entered
either the estimated slope or the centring constant. Per-gene coefficients,
p-values and coefficients of determination were retained.

Two properties of this adjustment merit explicit statement. Diagnosis was not
included in the age model. Because age and diagnosis are strongly correlated
in the training set (Section 3.1), the fitted age coefficient absorbs part of
the diagnosis effect, and subtracting it removes some disease-associated
variation alongside the age trend. The adjustment is therefore conservative,
biasing against the detection of signal rather than towards it. The
adjustment is also linear, so any non-linear age dependence is retained.

An earlier implementation fitted the age effect across all 62 samples,
including held-out samples. This was identified during the study as a leak
and corrected; the leaked implementation and all matrices derived from it are
superseded and are not used here.

2.4 Data partitioning

The training and held-out sets were formed by stratified random partition on
diagnosis, with a test fraction of 0.20 and a fixed random seed, yielding 49
training and 13 held-out samples.

2.5 Model development and selection history

The final model was an L1-penalised logistic regression (liblinear solver,
inverse regularisation strength C = 0.3, maximum 10,000 iterations, fixed
random seed) over ABCA6, CRLF1 and TNFRSF11B, with feature standardisation
fitted on training data only and applied to external data using
training-derived parameters. Fitted coefficients were 0.475 for ABCA6, 0.439
for TNFRSF11B and 0.380 for CRLF1. Model coefficients, scaler parameters and
the decision threshold were locked prior to transfer testing, and the
transfer cohort was scored without refitting.

This subsection is required for correct interpretation of every transfer-test
value reported below.

At least three model generations preceded the final specification: a
fifteen-gene signature, a seven-gene model comprising the final three genes
plus PLA2G7, SORBS1, PMS2P2 and TAB2 (internal ROC-AUC approximately 0.833,
transfer ROC-AUC approximately 0.269), and the final three-gene model.
Analysis-script modification times place the first extraction of GSE5281 and
the first transfer evaluation on the evening of one day, followed the next
morning by an uninterrupted rebuild of the discovery pipeline in strict
sequence: raw CEL preprocessing, quality control, annotation, the
training-test partition, the variance filter, the age adjustment, the LASSO
feature selection that produced the three genes, and the regularisation
tuning that fixed C = 0.3. The partition, feature set and regularisation
parameter were therefore all fixed after the transfer cohort had been
examined.

Accordingly, the transfer ROC-AUC reported in this study is not an unbiased
out-of-sample estimate. We report it as a selection-conditioned transfer
estimate and draw no inference about generalisation from it. We regard this
disclosure as material rather than incidental: it is a contemporaneously
documented instance of validation optimism arising in an analysis explicitly
attempting to avoid it, and it is the reason the present study treats the
transfer test as an object of investigation rather than as evidence for the
signature.

Separate audit analyses used default L2-penalised logistic regression and are
not the canonical performance model. The difference between the L2
out-of-fold estimate of 0.835 and the apparent L1 training value of 0.855
reflects regularisation, not data. Analyses using a superseded age-adjusted
matrix and an earlier data partition are excluded from this report.

2.6 Internal evaluation

Discrimination was summarised by ROC-AUC and average precision. Internal
performance was assessed on the training data, on the held-out test set, and
by repeated nested cross-validation using five outer folds and fifty repeats,
giving 250 iterations, with feature selection performed inside outer training
folds.

Feature-selection stability was assessed by three procedures: LASSO stability
selection over repeated folds, LASSO bootstrap positive-coefficient
frequency, and nested cross-validation selection frequency. Only the last is
free of selection leakage, and it is treated as the primary estimate.

2.7 Transfer test, calibration and uncertainty

The locked model was applied to the GSE5281 hippocampal expression matrix.
Raw predictions were evaluated first, and harmonisation to the training
reference, in which each gene's external values were rescaled to the training
mean and standard deviation, was assessed as a sensitivity analysis.

Discrimination and calibration were assessed separately. Calibration was
summarised by Brier score and by calibration intercept and slope, the latter
obtained by clipping predicted probabilities at 1 x 10^-6, taking the logit,
and fitting a binomial generalised linear model of outcome on logit predicted
probability. Because external predicted probabilities fall near 0.003, the
logit lies near -5.8 and the fitted intercept is sensitive to the clipping
constant, which is therefore reported explicitly. Threshold-based
classification used the locked decision threshold.

Uncertainty was estimated by bootstrap resampling and by permutation of
diagnosis labels using 10,000 permutations, performed separately for the raw
and harmonised analyses.

2.8 Identifiability and cohort-structure audit

Sample-level metadata were reconstructed from the GEO SOFT record for all 161
GSE5281 samples, using submission date as the cohort indicator. A design
matrix containing an intercept, diagnosis and submission cohort was
constructed and its rank computed.

Transcriptome-wide structure was examined by principal component analysis of
standardised expression, with component-diagnosis association assessed by
Mann-Whitney U test, Spearman correlation and ROC-AUC. Genes with missing
values or zero variance were removed before scaling. A separate
transcriptome-wide logistic classifier was trained to distinguish submission
cohorts and evaluated by leave-one-out cross-validation.

Six workflow-related metadata fields, covering extraction, labelling,
labelling protocol, hybridisation, scanning and data processing, were
compared between cohorts. Because absent metadata cannot establish that
workflows differed, differential availability was interpreted as a failure to
confirm equivalence rather than as proof of a technical artefact.

2.9 Ablation and confounder analyses

All seven one-, two- and three-gene combinations were evaluated under the
locked specification, with paired bootstrap comparison of transfer ROC-AUC.
Age and sex were evaluated as standalone predictors and in joint models with
the signature, assessed by likelihood-ratio tests.

2.10 Cell-type composition

Cell-type composition was estimated using marker-score proxies rather than
deconvolution. Each marker gene was standardised across samples and the
per-cell-type score taken as the mean standardised value across markers
present in the matrix. Marker sets were defined for oligodendrocytes
(MBP, MOG, MOBP, PLP1, OLIG1, OLIG2, MAG, CLDN11, CNP, ERMN), astrocytes
(GFAP, AQP4, ALDH1L1, SLC1A3, GJA1, SOX9), microglia (AIF1, CX3CR1, CSF1R,
C1QA, C1QB, C1QC, P2RY12), neurons (RBFOX3, SNAP25, SYT1, MAP2, NEFL, ENO2),
endothelial cells (CLDN5, PECAM1, VWF, KDR, EMCN, ESAM) and oligodendrocyte
precursor cells (PDGFRA, CSPG4, SOX10, OLIG1, OLIG2).

OLIG1 and OLIG2 appear in both the oligodendrocyte and precursor marker sets,
so these two scores share two markers and are not independent. This is
disclosed as a limitation of the comparator set.

2.11 Annotation and network analysis

The three genes were submitted to STRING for interaction and functional
enrichment analysis, and to Gene Ontology, KEGG and Reactome. Because the
query set contains three genes, enrichment statistics computed on it are
dominated by single-gene overlaps. These results are therefore reported as
gene-level annotation rather than pathway enrichment, and odds ratios and
combined scores derived from single-gene overlaps are not reported.

2.12 Software

Analyses were performed in Python using scikit-learn 1.9.0, numpy 2.5.2,
pandas 2.3.3, scipy 1.17.1, statsmodels 0.14.6, xgboost 3.4.1, shap 0.52.0
and gseapy 1.3.1, with figures produced using matplotlib 3.11.1 and seaborn
0.13.2. Preprocessing, variance filtering and age adjustment were performed
in R. A fixed random seed was used throughout.



3. RESULTS


3.1 The discovery cohort is confounded by age

The discovery cohort is age-imbalanced to a degree that limits
interpretation independently of anything observed in the transfer cohort
(Table 1). In the training set, cases had a mean age of 82.5 years (SD 9.0,
range 60-94) and controls a mean age of 62.5 years (SD 24.2, range 20-96);
the control group includes individuals as young as 20 years. Age alone
achieved a training ROC-AUC of 0.741 and a held-out ROC-AUC of 0.708.

In the transfer cohort, by contrast, ages are approximately balanced (cases
77.8, SD 5.7; controls 79.6, SD 9.4), and age alone achieved a ROC-AUC of
0.423.

The signature carried information beyond age within the discovery data
(likelihood-ratio chi-square 25.53, 1 degree of freedom, p = 4.36 x 10^-7),
and age likewise added information to the signature (chi-square 13.86,
p = 1.97 x 10^-4). These tests establish non-redundancy; they do not
establish that the discovery signal is free of age-related structure. The
model was developed in a setting where age tracks diagnosis and applied in a
setting where it does not.

3.2 Internal performance and selection stability

The final signature comprised ABCA6, TNFRSF11B and CRLF1. Apparent training
ROC-AUC was 0.855, with an average precision of 0.828. Held-out ROC-AUC was
0.694, with an average precision of 0.694, computed on four cases and nine
controls and therefore on 36 discordant pairs.

Repeated nested cross-validation gave a mean ROC-AUC of 0.757 (median 0.762,
SD 0.165), with a 2.5th to 97.5th percentile range of 0.398 to 1.000
(Figure 4). A substantial fraction of outer folds fell below 0.5.

Feature-selection stability was strongly procedure-dependent (Table 2).
Nested cross-validation selection frequencies, the only estimates free of
selection leakage, were 0.628 for TNFRSF11B, 0.504 for CRLF1 and 0.440 for
ABCA6, with ABCA6 ranking sixth among candidate genes. LASSO stability
selection over repeated folds gave 0.52, 0.52 and 0.50 respectively, and
bootstrap positive-coefficient frequencies gave 0.152, 0.057 and 0.092. Only
TNFRSF11B stands out under any procedure, and none of the three genes is
stably selected.

3.3 Transfer-test discrimination

Applied to the 23 GSE5281 hippocampal samples, the locked model achieved a
raw transfer ROC-AUC of 0.662 with an average precision of 0.603.
Harmonisation to the training reference gave 0.685 and 0.635 respectively.
The difference of 0.023 corresponds, at this sample size, to three of 130
discordant pairs.

Bootstrap resampling of the harmonised analysis gave a 95% interval of 0.442
to 0.886. Permutation testing against 10,000 label permutations gave p =
0.206 for the raw analysis and p = 0.145 for the harmonised analysis. The
permutation null had a mean of 0.498 and a 2.5th to 97.5th percentile range
of 0.254 to 0.746, so both observed values fall inside the null range.

Under the same locked specification, ablation showed that the combination of
ABCA6 and TNFRSF11B achieved a higher transfer ROC-AUC (0.731) than the full
three-gene model (0.662) (Table 3). Paired bootstrap comparison gave an
observed difference of 0.069 in favour of the reduced model, with a 95%
interval of -0.159 to 0.278 and a posterior probability of 0.736 that the
difference is positive. Superiority of the three-gene model is therefore not
supported, and neither is superiority of the reduced model. We note that the
reduced model's higher transfer value must not be used to justify post-hoc
panel reduction, as doing so would compound the selection dependency
described in Section 2.5.

3.4 Diagnosis is perfectly confounded with submission cohort

All thirteen hippocampal control samples belong to the July 2006 submission
cohort, and all ten hippocampal case samples belong to the October 2007
cohort (Figure 1a). Diagnosis is perfectly nested within submission cohort.

A design matrix containing an intercept, diagnosis and submission cohort has
dimensions 23 by 3 and rank 2 (Figure 1b). The design is rank deficient and
the diagnosis vector equals the submission indicator. Diagnosis and cohort
effects cannot be separately estimated.

This structure is not confined to the hippocampal subset. Across the full
161-sample series, the July 2006 submission comprises 74 samples, every one
of which is titled as a control, spanning entorhinal cortex, hippocampus,
middle temporal gyrus, posterior cingulate, superior frontal gyrus and
primary visual cortex. The October 2007 submission comprises 87 samples,
every one of which is titled as affected, spanning the same six regions.
Every case-control contrast available in GSE5281, in any brain region, is
confounded with submission cohort in the same manner.

The two submissions also employ different tissue vocabularies, with controls
annotated as "Medial Temporal Gyrus" and "Posterior Cingulate" and cases as
"Middle temporal gyrus" and "Posterior cingulate cortex", consistent with
separate submission and annotation events.

3.5 Transcriptome-wide cohort separation

Principal component analysis of the hippocampal subset showed the first
component explaining 26.97% of variance and the second 9.40%. The first
component was strongly associated with diagnosis (ROC-AUC 0.115, equivalent
to 0.885 with the sign reversed; Mann-Whitney p = 0.0021; Spearman rho
-0.661, p = 5.9 x 10^-4), as was the second (ROC-AUC 0.908; Mann-Whitney
p = 0.0011; rho 0.701, p = 2.0 x 10^-4). No later component showed
association, the third giving a ROC-AUC of 0.415.

More directly, a transcriptome-wide logistic classifier assigned all 23
samples to their correct submission cohort under leave-one-out
cross-validation, with a ROC-AUC and accuracy of 1.000 (Figure 1c). The two
submission cohorts are perfectly separable from expression data alone.

Six workflow-related metadata fields were documented for the case submission
and absent for the control submission. The case submission records data
processing as MAS5.0 with target scaling to 150, extraction using the
Arcturus PicoPure system, and labelling by double-round expression
profiling. Equivalence of experimental workflow between the two cohorts
therefore cannot be confirmed from the deposited record.

3.6 The two datasets do not profile the same measurement target

GSE5281 profiles laser-capture microdissected, non-tangle-bearing neurons
with two-round linear amplification, consistent with the deposited extraction
and labelling protocols described above. GSE48350 profiles bulk hippocampal
tissue.

The consequences are visible in the expression distributions (Figure 2). The
GSE48350 densities are broad and approximately symmetric with a mode near 6.5
log2 units, whereas the GSE5281 densities are sharply unimodal near 2.7 with
a long right tail. The difference is one of distributional shape rather than
location, which a difference in scanning batch or scaling factor would not
produce.

Variance compression is also severe and gene-specific (Table 4). Training
standard deviations were 0.94 for ABCA6, 0.84 for CRLF1 and 0.80 for
TNFRSF11B, whereas the corresponding raw transfer standard deviations were
0.15, 0.56 and 0.16. Harmonisation to the training reference restores the
marginal moments by construction but does not render the underlying
measurements comparable.

3.7 Discrimination transported; calibration did not

In the discovery data the model was reasonably calibrated. Training mean
predicted probability was 0.374 against an observed prevalence of 0.306, with
a Brier score of 0.137 and a calibration intercept of -0.009. In the held-out
set, mean predicted probability was 0.461 against an observed prevalence of
0.308, with a Brier score of 0.218; the calibration intercept of -0.715 and
slope of 1.089 are unstable at n = 13.

In the transfer cohort, calibration failed completely (Figure 3). Predicted
case probabilities ranged from 0.0019 to 0.0068, with a mean of 0.0029 and a
median of 0.0026, against an observed prevalence of 0.435. The Brier score
was 0.432. The locked threshold of 0.330 was unreachable by two orders of
magnitude, and all 23 samples were classified as control, giving a
sensitivity of 0.000, a specificity of 1.000, a negative predictive value of
0.565 and an undefined positive predictive value. Rank discrimination was
nevertheless preserved at a ROC-AUC of 0.662.

Harmonisation acted principally as a calibration correction rather than a
discrimination gain, reducing the Brier score from 0.432 to 0.229 while
changing ROC-AUC by 0.023. The threshold-based metrics for the raw transfer
model describe a degenerate all-negative classifier and should not be
interpreted as performance.

3.8 Cell-type composition

Within the discovery cohort, the signature was not reducible to the evaluated
composition scores. The three-gene score achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.855 and
remained at 0.861 after residualisation for oligodendrocyte score.
Oligodendrocyte score alone achieved 0.549, and adding it to the signature
changed ROC-AUC by approximately zero. The signature added information over
the first two composition principal components (chi-square 10.94,
p = 9.4 x 10^-4), and no individual cell-type score added information over
the signature, all p exceeding 0.22.

The signature was not, however, shown to improve discrimination over
composition. The observed difference was 0.037 with a 95% interval of -0.19
to 0.24. In a separate cross-validated analysis using default L2
regularisation, astrocyte score alone achieved discrimination comparable to
the signature. A joint model containing all six composition scores was not
estimable owing to a singular Hessian, with approximately 2.5 events per
variable.

ABCA6 correlated positively with oligodendrocyte score (Spearman rho 0.372,
p = 0.0085), whereas CRLF1 and TNFRSF11B did not (rho -0.106, p = 0.467 and
rho -0.065, p = 0.658 respectively).

This creates a coherence problem, which we state rather than resolve. The
composition association observed for ABCA6 is an oligodendrocyte association
measured in bulk tissue. The transfer cohort consists of laser-captured
neurons from which glia have been deliberately excluded. Whatever produced
transfer discrimination in GSE5281, it cannot be the oligodendrocyte-
associated biology observed in the discovery cohort.

3.9 Single-cell and cross-tissue behaviour

In GSE138852, TNFRSF11B was absent from the expression matrix entirely, so
only two of the three genes could be assessed. ABCA6 and CRLF1 were detected
in 4.0% and 4.1% of cells respectively, at the limit of detection.

Pseudobulk comparison across three case and three control libraries found
ABCA6 higher in case oligodendrocytes (0.128 against 0.021, a 6.06-fold
difference, Cohen's d 1.59), but with an exact permutation p of 0.2 and a
false discovery rate of 0.80. No comparison in this analysis survived
multiple-testing correction; the smallest false discovery rate across all
tested gene and cell-type combinations was 0.80.

Oligodendrocyte proportion differed markedly between conditions in the same
libraries, at 60.0%, 64.6% and 79.7% in cases against 36.5%, 39.4% and 47.0%
in controls, so composition and the ABCA6 signal vary in the same direction.
The library-level correlation between oligodendrocyte percentage and ABCA6
expression was rho 0.43 (p = 0.40, n = 6). The single-cell data are therefore
not inconsistent with the discovery observation, but they are underpowered
and confounded with composition, and cannot be described as independent
validation.

In blood, ABCA6 added modestly to a model containing age and sex, raising
ROC-AUC from 0.631 to 0.682, a difference of 0.051, with a likelihood-ratio
p of approximately 4.2 x 10^-4 and Akaike information criterion falling from
289.2 to 278.8. Effect directions across tissues diverged. ABCA6 and CRLF1
retained sign, with Cohen's d of 1.089, 0.551 and 0.137 and of 1.055, 0.059
and 0.098 across discovery, transfer and blood respectively, whereas
TNFRSF11B reversed direction in blood, with values of 1.221, 0.345 and -0.372
(Figure 5).

3.10 Annotation and network analysis

STRING returned no interactions among ABCA6, CRLF1 and TNFRSF11B and no
functional enrichment for the three-gene set. The genes do not constitute a
connected module.

First-shell neighbours were gene-specific. CRLF1 associated with CLCF1
(confidence 0.999), IL27 (0.982), CNTFR (0.870) and CNTF (0.856); ABCA6 with
ABCA9 (0.790) and ABCA8 (0.683); and TNFRSF11B with TNFRSF11A (0.762) and
IL17RC (0.623).

Gene Ontology, KEGG and Reactome results reflect single-gene annotations: ABC
transporters (one of 45 genes, adjusted p = 0.020, ABCA6), osteoclast
differentiation (one of 127, adjusted p = 0.028, TNFRSF11B),
cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (one of 295, adjusted p = 0.044,
TNFRSF11B) and ciliary neurotrophic factor receptor binding (one of five,
adjusted p = 0.0037, CRLF1). These are annotations rather than enrichment and
provide no evidence that the three genes act in a shared pathway.




4. DISCUSSION



4.1 The confounding structure and its relation to previous work

We found that in GSE5281 diagnosis is perfectly nested within submission
cohort, across the whole series and in every profiled region; that the
resulting design is rank deficient; and that transcriptome-wide expression
separates the two submission cohorts without error.

Batch effects in public expression data are thoroughly documented and their
correction is standard practice. Several published studies merge GSE5281 with
GSE48350 and other series using surrogate variable analysis or ComBat before
analysis [5], and others use GSE5281 as a validation cohort. We were unable to
locate a published report of the submission-cohort nesting itself.

Our finding does not contradict the batch-effect literature. It identifies a
case in which the standard remedy is inapplicable. ComBat and surrogate
variable analysis estimate batch effects conditional on the biological
variable of interest; when batch and biology are collinear, the conditional
model is not identified, and adjustment removes the disease contrast together
with the technical one. This is a limitation of the design rather than of the
software.

The most likely explanation is straightforward study logistics: controls were
profiled and deposited in 2006 and cases in 2007. The two submissions carry
different tissue vocabularies and, for the case submission, explicitly
documented MAS5 processing. Sequential collection of cases and controls was
unremarkable in that period. The problem lies in downstream reuse that treats
the resulting series as a single balanced cohort.

What our study adds is a specific and checkable statement about a heavily
reused resource, supported by a formal identifiability argument and a
transcriptome-wide demonstration rather than a diagnostic plot. Any reader
can verify the claim directly from the deposited SOFT record.

What remains uncertain is how much of any observed case-control difference in
GSE5281 is biological. That is precisely the point: within this design, the
quantity is not estimable. We do not claim that differential expression
reported from GSE5281 is artefactual. We claim that it is not separable from
cohort.

4.2 What transfer performance does and does not indicate

The locked model achieved a transfer ROC-AUC of 0.662, or 0.685 after
harmonisation, with a bootstrap interval spanning 0.442 to 0.886 and
permutation p values of 0.206 and 0.145.

Performance attenuation on genuinely external data is the norm in this
literature. Lee and Lee reported internal five-fold ROC-AUC values of 0.657
to 0.874 across ADNI and AddNeuroMed blood cohorts, falling to 0.619 to 0.859
on cross-dataset evaluation [9]. Lunnon and colleagues reported a 48-gene
blood classifier achieving 75% accuracy, outperformed by magnetic resonance
imaging at 85% in the same subjects [10]. Blood classifiers reaching ROC-AUC
0.886 have been reported in independent train-test designs [11].

Our transfer value sits at the low end of this range, which is unsurprising
given a training set of 49 samples with 15 events and three predictors,
approximately five events per variable and below conventional guidance. We
make no claim of comparative performance. The transfer estimate is
selection-conditioned, the two cohorts differ in measurement target, and the
sample sizes are too small for the comparison to be informative. Reporting
the number alongside published values without these qualifications would be
precisely the practice this study argues against.

What our study adds is a demonstration that a transfer ROC-AUC in the range
routinely described as acceptable can arise in a cohort where the disease
effect is not identifiable, where the measurement target differs, and where
the probability scale has collapsed.

4.3 Measurement-target mismatch

GSE5281 profiles laser-captured neurons with two-round amplification and
GSE48350 profiles bulk tissue. Expression distributions differ in shape, and
per-gene variance in the transfer cohort is compressed by up to sixfold.

That laser capture and bulk profiling measure different quantities is not in
dispute; it is the stated rationale for the GSE5281 design, which was
intended to isolate neuronal signal from surrounding tissue. The importance
of cellular heterogeneity in bulk brain expression is likewise well
established, and single-nucleus atlases have been developed in part to
address it [12].

We agree with this literature and extend it to validation practice. A study
that treats GSE5281 as an external validation cohort for a bulk-derived
signature is not testing generalisation across cohorts; it is testing
transfer across measurement modalities, without stating that this is what it
is doing.

Amplification and cell-type purification both alter the marginal distribution
and compress between-sample variance. Mean-variance harmonisation to a
training reference restores marginal moments arithmetically but does not
restore comparability. What our study adds is a concrete mechanism for the
calibration failure and a specific caution about a common dataset pairing.

4.4 Discrimination and calibration are separate properties

We observed a mean predicted case probability of 0.003 against an observed
prevalence of 0.435, with all samples classified as control, while ROC-AUC
remained at 0.662.

The distinction between discrimination and calibration is standard in
clinical prediction modelling, and systematic appraisals of Alzheimer's
disease risk models have found that most published studies are
methodologically deficient with a high risk of bias, and that external
validation is uncommon [13]. Our result agrees with and illustrates that
literature. Its particular value lies in the magnitude: a discrepancy of two
orders of magnitude is not a subtle miscalibration but a clear signal that
the model is being applied to data unlike anything it was fitted on.

What our study adds is an argument for reporting calibration as a diagnostic
for distributional mismatch rather than as a secondary performance property.
The collapsed probability scale would have flagged the measurement-target
problem before any metadata audit was undertaken.

4.5 The signature genes

We do not present ABCA6, CRLF1 or TNFRSF11B as Alzheimer's disease
biomarkers, and our evidence does not support doing so.

We found no established association between ABCA6 and Alzheimer's disease. We
note explicitly that ABCA6 is not ABCA7. ABCA7 is among the best-supported
Alzheimer's disease risk genes across genomics, transcriptomics and
methylomics [14], and framing ABCA6 within a general ABC transporter
narrative risks borrowing that support illegitimately. Our evidence for
ABCA6 comprises a discovery-cohort effect size of 1.09, a correlation with
oligodendrocyte score of 0.372, a single-cell oligodendrocyte difference at a
false discovery rate of 0.80 confounded with composition, and a nested
selection frequency of 0.440 with a rank of sixth. This is weak.

We found no Alzheimer's disease literature for CRLF1. Its annotation places
it in interleukin-6 family and ciliary neurotrophic factor receptor
signalling. Its discovery effect size of 1.06 did not transport, falling to
0.06, and it contributed nothing to transfer discrimination: adding CRLF1 to
ABCA6 and TNFRSF11B reduced transfer ROC-AUC from 0.731 to 0.662.

TNFRSF11B is the one gene with independent literature of relevance.
Osteoprotegerin has been reported in cerebrospinal fluid as correlating with
translocator protein positron emission tomography signal in Alzheimer's
disease, in an explicitly exploratory analysis [15]. This is a different
analyte class in a different compartment, and so constitutes biological
plausibility rather than replication. TNFRSF11B was the most consistently
selected of our three genes, with a nested frequency of 0.628, but reversed
direction in blood.

None of these genes constitutes a defensible novel Alzheimer's disease
biomarker on our evidence. They are reported because the signature is the
vehicle for the methodological argument, not because we consider them
promising.

4.6 Claims not made

We do not claim that the observed transfer discrimination is entirely
technical in origin. We do not claim that the three genes lack biological
relevance. We do not claim that differential expression published from
GSE5281 is wrong. We do not claim clinical validity, mechanistic insight, or
superiority over any published model.

We claim that within GSE5281 diagnosis and submission cohort are not
separately identifiable; that the dataset profiles a different measurement
target from the bulk-tissue datasets with which it is merged and against
which signatures are validated; and that transfer performance measured under
these conditions cannot be interpreted as evidence of transportable
disease-associated signal.

4.7 Recommendations

Studies using GSE5281 as a case-control resource should state that diagnosis
and submission cohort are collinear, and that batch adjustment cannot recover
identifiability from a fully confounded design. Studies merging GSE5281 with
bulk-tissue series should state that the series differ in measurement target
and justify the merge. Cross-dataset evaluations should report calibration
alongside discrimination, treating a collapsed probability scale as
diagnostic information rather than a footnote. Model-selection history should
be reported, and where an evaluation cohort has influenced any modelling
decision, subsequent performance on that cohort should be described as
selection-conditioned. Finally, before a dataset is adopted as a validation
cohort, its design should be checked for collinearity between the outcome and
any administrative or technical variable available in the metadata. This
check takes minutes.




5. LIMITATIONS

The discovery cohort is small, comprising 49 training samples with 15 events,
and is severely age-imbalanced, with controls spanning ages 20 to 96. The
held-out test set contains 13 samples and therefore 36 discordant pairs, and
the transfer cohort contains 23 samples and 130 discordant pairs. At these
sample sizes the ROC-AUC statistic is coarse; the transfer AUC changes in
increments of 1/130. Every performance difference reported here lies within
sampling noise, and the nested cross-validation interval of 0.398 to 1.000 is
essentially uninformative about the model's true discrimination.

The transfer estimate is selection-conditioned, as described in Section 2.5.
The age adjustment is linear and omits diagnosis from the covariate model, so
it removes some disease-associated variation; this is conservative, but it
means the reported discovery performance is a lower bound relative to an
unadjusted model. The oligodendrocyte and precursor marker sets share two
genes, so those comparators are not independent, and composition estimates
are marker-score proxies rather than deconvolution.

We have not audited GSE48350 for the same submission-cohort structure. Sample
correlation structure in that dataset suggests grouping, and this analysis
should be completed.

Finally, this study examines a single dataset pair. We do not know how common
perfect outcome-cohort collinearity is across public repositories, and we do
not claim that it is typical.



6. CONCLUSION



We set out to test whether a compact transcriptomic signature developed in
one Alzheimer's disease brain cohort would transfer to another, and found
that the question could not be answered with the data available. In GSE5281,
diagnosis is perfectly nested within submission cohort across the entire
series; the corresponding design is rank deficient; and transcriptome-wide
expression separates the submission cohorts without error. The dataset also
profiles laser-captured neurons rather than bulk tissue, so transfer into it
crosses a measurement boundary as well as a cohort boundary. Under these
conditions our locked model produced a transfer ROC-AUC of 0.662, a value
that would ordinarily be reported as modest external validation, while
assigning a mean case probability of 0.003 to a cohort with 43.5%
prevalence.

The specific signature is unremarkable and we make no claim for it. The
general point is that discrimination, identifiability and calibration are
three separate properties, and that measuring the first says nothing about
the other two. A transfer test performed without checking design
identifiability and probability calibration is not validation, and reporting
it as such is a substantive error rather than a matter of wording.
